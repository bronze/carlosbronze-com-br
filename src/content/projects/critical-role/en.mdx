## The itch

I watch a lot of [Critical Role](https://critrole.com/), and their homepage works like a catalog: everything carries the same weight, and visitors have to figure out on their own where to start.

What bothered me most was the most basic question a regular viewer has: what's airing this week, and when? On the current site, the schedule is confusing and hard to find. And anyone who wants to understand the campaigns, who the characters are and where to start watching, has to dig for it too.

## The insight

The page itself asks "How do you want to do this?", Matt Mercer's most famous line. But the answers are Games, Shows, Books and Charity: the company's departments. The homepage was organized around Critical Role's org chart, not around visitors' questions. The weekly schedule, the thing I most wanted to find, was there, but as a carousel image at the top and a list way down the page, after eight banners.

So I put myself in the user's shoes, which in this case were my own, and solved my own pain first.

## What I did

I started by reviewing the site's information architecture, not its look. Instead of treating the page as a catalog, I structured the navigation as a decision system, making clear what people need first, what they explore next, and where the subscription fits in naturally.

## The result

A simpler, more intentional structure that lowers cognitive load and does a better job of guiding engagement: each block on the page answers a visitor's question, in the order it comes up.

## What I learned

> The first thing on the page should answer the question people ask most, not the one the company most wants to answer.

Order matters as much as content. Part of the work was creating what was missing, but a lot of it was deciding what comes first. And the subscription works better once the page has already answered the visitor's question.
